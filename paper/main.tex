\begin{abstract}
Tamil has tens of millions of speakers, yet none of the open speech-to-speech models we surveyed in
October 2026 accepts or produces Tamil speech. We document this gap and take the first steps
toward \emph{Oosai} (\ta{ஓசை}, ``sound''), a Tamil voice agent built from open, commercially usable
parts. We (i)~survey open and announced speech-to-speech systems and the Tamil-capable components
available today; (ii)~report a reproducible head-to-head of two open multilingual Indian speech
recognizers, IndicConformer-600M and Indic-Transcribe-core, on the FLEURS Tamil test set, run on a
consumer Apple-Silicon laptop; (iii)~show that the two trade off rather than one dominating:
Indic-Transcribe makes significantly fewer word errors on clean clips (1.8 points, 95\% CI
0.8--2.7), while IndicConformer makes significantly fewer character errors over the full set
(1.0 point, CI 0.2--2.0); (iv)~identify two properties of the benchmark that inflate Tamil error
rates: 22 of 591 clips whose audio contains about twice as much speech per reference character as
the rest, consistent with speaker repetitions missing from the references, and orthographic
variation such as word-final sandhi consonants, which alone accounts for 2.6 points of word error
rate for both systems; and (v)~analyze component licenses and estimate the compute needed for an
end-to-end Tamil speech-to-speech model. We release the evaluation code, all per-utterance outputs,
and the text of a 30-utterance colloquial, code-mixed test set spanning six application domains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Spoken conversational agents have moved from cascades of separate recognition, language and
synthesis models toward single models that listen and speak directly, some of them full-duplex
\citep{defossez2024moshi,fang2024llamaomni,wang2024freezeomni,xu2025qwen3omni,cui2026minicpmo}.
Their language coverage, however, is narrow. Tamil, an official language in India, Sri Lanka and
Singapore with tens of millions of speakers, is absent from the speech input and output languages
of every open speech-to-speech (S2S) model we examined (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).

This report describes the groundwork for \emph{Oosai}, a Tamil voice agent intended to cover customer
support, personal assistance, booking, education, health information and casual conversation. We
start from a cascade of Tamil-trained open components rather than a single model, because every
part of the cascade already exists for Tamil and each can be measured and replaced independently.
A single Tamil S2S model is the longer-term goal; we estimate its cost in Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}.

This is a work-in-progress report. \textbf{We have not trained any model.} Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A snapshot survey (October 2026) of open and announced S2S systems with respect to Tamil,
        and of the Tamil-capable recognition, language and synthesis components (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
  \item A reproducible comparison of IndicConformer-600M \citep{ai4bharat2025indicconformer} and
        Indic-Transcribe-core \citep{bodhan2026indictranscribe} on FLEURS Tamil
        \citep{conneau2022fleurs}, with paired bootstrap confidence intervals and timing on an
        Apple M1 Max laptop (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}).
  \item An analysis of FLEURS Tamil reference issues and of how Tamil orthography inflates word error
        rate, with a recommendation to report character error rate and sandhi-insensitive word
        error rate alongside standard word error rate (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
  \item A license analysis for commercial deployment and for releasing derived models
        (Section~\ref{sec:license}), and a costed roadmap to an end-to-end model
        (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}).
  \item A domain-diverse colloquial Tamil test set design with built-in safety cases
        (Section~\ref{sec:testset}, Appendix~\ref{app:testset}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{End-to-end spoken dialogue.}
Moshi \citep{defossez2024moshi} models user and system speech as parallel token streams for
full-duplex conversation. J-Moshi \citep{ohashi2025jmoshi} adapted it to Japanese by pre-training on
about 60k hours of Japanese dialogue and fine-tuning on 344 hours of stereo dialogue, the closest
published precedent for moving a full-duplex model to a new language. LLaMA-Omni
\citep{fang2024llamaomni} and Freeze-Omni \citep{wang2024freezeomni} attach a speech encoder and a
streaming speech decoder to a text LLM, training on synthesized spoken instruction data; LLaMA-Omni
used 200k speech instruction pairs and about three days on four GPUs. Qwen3-Omni
\citep{xu2025qwen3omni} and MiniCPM-o~4.5 \citep{cui2026minicpmo} are large omni-modal models with
speech output.

\paragraph{Indian-language speech resources.}
FLEURS \citep{conneau2022fleurs} provides read speech for 102 languages including Tamil. IndicVoices
\citep{javed2024indicvoices} collected natural and read speech across 22 Indian languages;
IndicVoices-R \citep{sankar2024indicvoicesr}, Rasa \citep{varadhan2024rasa} and Rasmalai
\citep{sankar2025rasmalai} target synthesis-quality and expressive speech.

\paragraph{Tamil-capable recognition and synthesis.}
IndicConformer-600M \citep{ai4bharat2025indicconformer} is a hybrid CTC/RNN-T Conformer for the 22
scheduled Indian languages. Indic-Transcribe-core \citep{bodhan2026indictranscribe} is a 1.2B-parameter
model on the Canary architecture \citep{sekoyan2025canary} covering 25 languages, with claimed
support for code-mixed speech. IndicF5 \citep{praveen2025indicf5}, built on F5-TTS
\citep{chen2024f5tts}, synthesizes 11 Indian languages from a short voice prompt; Indic-Speak
\citep{bodhan2026indicspeak} covers 22 Indian languages and English with a fixed library of voices.

\paragraph{Planned cascade.}
Oosai's first version chains voice activity detection, Tamil recognition, a Tamil-capable LLM with
tools (Sarvam-30B \citep{sarvam2026sarvam30b}, a mixture-of-experts model with about 2.4B active
parameters, is the first candidate), and Tamil synthesis (IndicF5 for consented voice cloning,
Indic-Speak for library voices). The cascade gives up full-duplex turn-taking and loses tone of
voice at the text bottleneck, in exchange for components that are already trained on Tamil and can
be tuned separately. Tamil-specific requirements include colloquial rather than literary register in
generated replies, robustness to Tamil--English code-mixing, dialect coverage, spelling out numbers,
dates and currency before synthesis, and an explicit choice between formal and familiar address.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data.} The FLEURS Tamil (\texttt{ta\_in}) test split: 591 utterances, 2.13 hours,
16\,kHz mono, read sentences. We score against the dataset's normalized \texttt{transcription}
field. Nine clips are longer than 30\,s and four longer than 45\,s (maximum 60\,s).

\paragraph{Systems.} \emph{IndicConformer-600M}: the released ONNX export, loaded through its
Hugging Face remote code, RNN-T decoding with language \texttt{ta}, on CPU with onnxruntime 1.20.1
(newer onnxruntime versions refuse its externally stored weights when loaded from the Hugging Face
cache). \emph{Indic-Transcribe-core}: the released PyTorch code on the Apple GPU (MPS) in float32,
language \texttt{ta}, one decoding pass for clips up to 30\,s and the model's own pause-based
chunker for longer clips.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Both reference and hypothesis are Unicode NFC-normalized and lower-cased;
characters in Unicode punctuation and symbol categories are replaced by spaces and whitespace is
collapsed. Tamil vowel signs (categories Mn/Mc) are kept. Word error rate (WER) is computed over
whitespace tokens; character error rate (CER) over Unicode code points including spaces, so a
consonant with a vowel sign counts as two characters. Both are corpus-level. We compare systems with
a paired bootstrap over utterances (1,000 resamples, 95\% percentile intervals).

\paragraph{Timing and hardware.} Apple M1 Max with 64\,GB memory, macOS 27.0.1, Python 3.11,
PyTorch 2.15, transformers 5.21. We report the median wall-clock time per utterance, excluding model
loading, and the real-time factor (RTF): total processing time divided by total audio duration. The
two systems ran on different processors (CPU versus GPU), so timings show feasibility on a laptop
rather than a controlled speed comparison.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Clips whose references omit speech}
\label{sec:suspect}

For 22 of the 591 clips (3.7\%), \emph{both} systems produce more than 1.3 times as many words as the
reference. In clip \texttt{002\_1666}, for example, both transcribe the sentence and then most of it a
second time, and Indic-Transcribe also transcribes a restart at the start of the repeat. Two
independent systems agreeing on the extra words suggests the extra speech is in the audio. A
check that does not depend on either system supports this: these clips contain a median 0.175\,s of
audio per reference character, against 0.080\,s for the other clips, about 2.2 times as much. The
most likely explanation is speakers repeating or restarting sentences, with the reference keeping
only one reading. \todo{confirm by listening to all 22 clips and list their IDs in an appendix.}

These clips matter for comparisons. Removing them lowers both systems' CER by more than four
points, and IndicConformer's significant CER advantage over the full set disappears, so that
advantage is concentrated in clips where the reference is likely incomplete.

\subsection{Orthographic variation inflates Tamil word error rate}

Tamil is agglutinative, so words are long and a single-character difference makes the whole word an
error. Many such differences are spelling conventions rather than recognition mistakes
(Table~\ref{tab:examples}). The most frequent are word-final doubled consonants from sandhi
(\ta{க்}, \ta{ச்}, \ta{த்}, \ta{ப்}), which writers include or omit inconsistently. Deleting
word-final \ta{க் ச் த் ப்} from both reference and hypothesis lowers WER by 2.6 points for both
systems (Table~\ref{tab:asr}), so about one in ten word errors is of this kind. Variant spellings of
the same word (\ta{ஆளுனர்} and \ta{ஆளுநர்}, both in use) and English words written in Latin script in
the reference but transliterated by the systems add further errors.

We therefore suggest that Tamil recognition results report CER and a sandhi-insensitive WER
alongside standard WER, and, where possible, an orthography-aware multi-reference metric of the kind
the Indic-Transcribe authors use.

Apache components. FLEURS is CC-BY~4.0, which permits releasing our per-utterance outputs. We

\section{Roadmap and compute estimate}
\label{sec:roadmap}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} estimates the GPU cost of three end-to-end designs. The figures are our
extrapolations from published runs, not measurements, at about US\$2.50 per H100-hour, and include
a factor of two to three for failed and tuning runs. LLaMA-Omni's run (200k pairs, about three days
on four GPUs) anchors option~B. J-Moshi's pre-training (128 V100 GPUs for about 36 hours, roughly
4,600 V100-hours, which we convert to about 1,200--1,500 H100-hours) anchors option~C, and Indic
Parler-TTS training in Rasmalai (32 H100s for 1.5 days) anchors the synthesis component.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering\small
\caption{Estimated GPU cost of an end-to-end Tamil model. Extrapolated, not measured.}
\label{tab:cost}
\begin{tabular}{p{3.2cm}p{5.6cm}cc}
\toprule
Design & Data needed & H100-hours & Cost (US\$) \\
\midrule
A. Speech-in LLM (encoder + LLM; separate synthesis) & 1--2k h transcribed Tamil + $\sim$50k synthetic spoken questions & 200--500 & 0.5k--1.5k \\
B. Turn-based S2S (LLaMA-Omni design) & $\sim$200k synthetic Tamil spoken question--answer pairs + 1k+ h clean Tamil speech & 1,500--4,000 & 4k--10k \\

\bottomrule

Compute is not the binding constraint for option~C: we know of no open Tamil conversational corpus
of tens of thousands of hours, and collecting one with consent would likely cost more than training.
Option~B can substitute synthetic dialogues generated by a Tamil-capable LLM and voiced by IndicF5.
Our plan is to run the cascade first, collect consented conversations through it, and then decide
whether option~B is justified. For task-oriented domains (support, booking, health information) we
expect the cascade to remain preferable, because its intermediate text can be checked and its tool
calls are reliable; a single model's main advantage is natural turn-taking in open conversation.

\section{A colloquial, multi-domain Tamil test set}
\label{sec:testset}

FLEURS does not test colloquial register, code-mixing or task requests. We drafted 30 utterances,
five in each of six domains (support, assistant, booking, education, health, casual chat), written
in spoken Chennai-style Tamil with English loanwords and numbers (Appendix~\ref{app:testset}).
English loanwords are written in Tamil script, matching how both recognizers output them. The same
utterances serve as prompts for evaluating the LLM; each carries a note on what a correct reply must
do. Health items include two safety cases: a medication-dose question the agent must not answer
with a dose, and a chest-pain report that must be answered with an instruction to call the 108
emergency number. \todo{native-speaker review of the wording; record the set with several speakers
and dialects; report recognition results on it.}

Planned evaluations not yet run: native-listener naturalness ratings for IndicF5 and Indic-Speak,
intelligibility measured by recognizing synthesized speech, LLM correctness and register on the
30 prompts, and end-to-end time to first audio (target: about two seconds).

\section{Limitations}

Our recognition results come from a single read-speech benchmark. The suspect clips are identified
by a heuristic and a speaking-time check, not by listening. The two systems ran on different
processors. We could not reproduce the vendor's benchmark. The survey is a snapshot and may be out
of date quickly. The compute estimates are extrapolations. No synthesis, language-model or
end-to-end results are reported yet.

\section{Ethics}

Voice cloning will use only voices whose speakers have given written consent. Recorded
conversations will be collected only with consent and handled in line with India's Digital
Personal Data Protection Act where it applies. The health domain is limited to general information,
appointment booking and referral to professionals, never diagnosis or dosing, and emergencies are
directed to emergency services; the Indic Open Model License also notes that its models are not
certified for medical use. Synthetic training data, if used, will be disclosed.
